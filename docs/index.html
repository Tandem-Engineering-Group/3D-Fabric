How this feeds the business goals

Prove the product

Prototypes → small batch → a real launch with real customers by week 10. Every purse-track task moves a bag from a render to something someone buys.

Fund it without debt

Amber Grant monthly entries plus one serious swing at Motor City Match (grants average ~$50k). Grant money buys materials, equipment, and batch capacity — not loan payments.

Build the audience

The book and the bags tell one Detroit story. Process photos feed the book; the book's excerpt feeds the launch; the launch feeds the grant narrative.

Keep the machine cheap

The pipeline already turns a sketch into a costed pattern in 17 seconds. Every new design is nearly free to spec — spend the money on fabric and thread, not drafting time.

The flywheel: the book builds the story → the story sells purses and strengthens grant applications → grants fund production → production gives the book its material. Each week's tasks push the same wheel.

First purse — the design brief

Erika: answer these ten in order — they move from the feeling to the form. Short answers are perfect; a voice memo counts. Then one page of sketching. The pipeline turns whatever you hand over into a 3D model, a pattern, and a cost sheet the same day.

1

How does she feel wearing it?

Three words for the woman carrying this bag. This is the whole design in miniature — everything else has to earn those three words.

e.g. “unstoppable, playful, put-together” · “soft, easy, warm”
2

Where is she when the bag is at its best?

One scene. The place tells us formality, weather, and how hard the bag works.

e.g. Eastern Market on a Saturday · gallery opening · airport gate · first date
3

What lives inside it?

List everything it must swallow. This decides pockets and honesty about size.

e.g. phone, keys, lipstick, sunglasses · or: laptop, water bottle, a paperback
4

How big, really?

Compare it to a real object instead of inches — we'll take the measurements from there.

e.g. “a vinyl record” · “a hardcover book” · “a lunchbox” · “a magazine”
5

How is it carried?

Shoulder, crossbody, in the hand, under the arm — or convertible? Strap length changes the whole silhouette.

e.g. “crossbody, hands free at the market, but a short handle too”
6

Does it hold its shape or slouch?

Structured like a frame bag, soft like a hobo, or somewhere between? This picks the materials' backbone.

e.g. “stands up on the table by itself” · “melts against the hip”
7

What's the color story?

Three colors max: the body, the accent, the hardware. Name the one loud thing — every good bag has exactly one.

e.g. “cognac body, cream stitch, brass” · “black on black, one coral zipper”
8

What does it feel like in the hand?

Close your eyes and touch it. Crisp canvas, buttery leather, structured felt, waxed and rugged?

e.g. “smooth and cool like a saddle” · “sturdy canvas that softens with age”
9

What story does it tell?

The theme. Collection 01 is all Detroit — is this bag part of that story, or its own thing?

e.g. “the Riverwalk at dusk” · “grandma's Sunday best” · “first snow on Second Ave”
10

What's the one detail people ask about?

The thing a stranger notices and says “where did you get that?” One detail, done perfectly, beats five done okay.

e.g. a painted edge · an unexpected lining print · a hand-stamped rivet · the stitch color

What to hand over (any format works)

  1. Answers to the ten questions — text, email, or a voice memo while you sketch.
  2. One page of sketches — front view and side view, pen on white paper. Wobbly lines are fine; proportions matter more than prettiness.
  3. A photo of the sketch with something for scale in frame (a coffee mug, your phone).
  4. Anything that inspires it — reference photos, a fabric swatch, a paint chip, a screenshot.

From there: sketch → 3D model → flat pattern → felt fit-check you can hold → real-fabric prototype. Weeks 1–4 of the plan.
